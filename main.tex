\section{Introduction}
Hier könnte Ihre Werbung stehen. Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), known under the brand name \textit{Teflon}\texttrademark, has become an essential material in rare-event search experiments due to its excellent optical and electrical properties. PTFE is commonly used in detectors, such as DARWIN, for its high reflectivity to vacuum ultraviolet light and its relatively low levels of radioactive contamination. However, radioactive impurities in PTFE can generate MeV-scale neutrons through spontaneous fission and ($\alpha$,n) reactions, which produce nuclear recoil backgrounds that interfere with the sensitive detection of weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) in the DARWIN experiment. 

To achieve DARWIN’s ambitious goal of detecting dark matter at unprecedented sensitivity levels, it is imperative to minimize radioactive bulk contaminations in PTFE to below the level of irreducible backgrounds, such as neutrino interactions. This study focuses on identifying commercially available PTFE with sufficient radiopurity to meet DARWIN's strict background requirements.

\section{Materials and Methods}
The aim of this study was to evaluate the radiopurity of PTFE samples from a variety of commercial suppliers in Europe. Eleven samples were obtained and screened using two complementary techniques:

\subsection{High-Purity Germanium Gamma Spectrometry (HPGe-GS)}
HPGe gamma spectrometry is a well-established method used to detect gamma rays emitted from radioactive decay chains in materials. This method is particularly sensitive to gamma-emitting isotopes from the late decay chains of uranium ($^{238}$U) and thorium ($^{232}$Th). The technique involves placing PTFE samples in close proximity to a germanium detector, which measures gamma emissions over an extended period (typically 30 days per sample). The sample is enclosed in a shielded environment to minimize ambient background radiation, and the gamma spectra are analyzed to determine contamination levels.

\subsection{Inductively-Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS)}
ICP-MS is a destructive method that provides high sensitivity for detecting trace amounts of uranium and thorium by directly measuring the isotopic composition of samples. The PTFE samples were first ashed, dissolved in acid, and then atomized in a high-temperature plasma. The ions were subsequently separated by their mass-to-charge ratio and detected. ICP-MS is particularly effective at measuring the early decay chains of $^{238}$U and $^{232}$Th, providing a complementary assessment to HPGe-GS, which is more sensitive to the late decay chains.

\subsection{Sample Selection and Preparation}
The PTFE samples were chosen from major European suppliers and represent a cross-sectional study of commercially available material. Each sample was either acquired pre-machined or prepared in the laboratory by cutting large stock plates into cuboid shapes. Solid samples were cleaned with ethanol before measurement to reduce surface contamination, and powder samples were stored in multiple layers of plastic bags. The specific activities of radioactive isotopes in each sample were measured and compared to literature values from previous dark matter search experiments, such as XENON and LZ.

\subsection{HPGe-GS Measurements}
The HPGe-GS measurements revealed detectable levels of contamination in several samples, particularly from the late decay chains of uranium and thorium. In some cases, the contamination was significant enough to generate substantial radiogenic neutron yields. For instance, samples with high levels of $^{226}$Ra and $^{228}$Th are of particular concern due to their ability to produce neutrons through ($\alpha$,n) reactions with fluorine, a major component of PTFE.

The background neutron contribution from these reactions was quantified for each sample, with a focus on the potential impact on DARWIN’s sensitivity. Samples with contamination levels exceeding $10^{-4}$ Bq/kg for $^{226}$Ra were found to contribute significantly to the nuclear recoil background. Several samples exceeded this threshold, posing a risk to the successful operation of the DARWIN detector.

\subsection{ICP-MS Measurements}
The ICP-MS results showed a range of contamination levels across the samples. The early parts of the uranium and thorium decay chains, measured directly through their parent isotopes $^{238}$U and $^{232}$Th, were detected in varying concentrations. In some cases, discrepancies were observed between the ICP-MS and HPGe-GS results, indicating potential breaks in secular equilibrium, where the early and late decay chain activities are not in alignment.

For example, sample PTFE8 exhibited a significant difference between the $^{238}$U content (as measured by ICP-MS) and the $^{226}$Ra content (as measured by HPGe-GS), suggesting that the manufacturing process may have introduced additional contamination during polymerization or sintering.

\subsection{Summary of Measurement Data}
The specific activities of uranium-238, thorium-232, and potassium-40, along with anthropogenic isotopes such as cobalt-60 and cesium-137, were determined for all PTFE samples using both high-purity germanium gamma spectrometry and inductively-coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). A comparison of the results between the two techniques provides a comprehensive understanding of the radiopurity levels across the examined samples. Table \ref{tab:data} (to be added later) summarizes the measurements for all isotopes of interest.

\subsection{Validation of Data}
The results demonstrate strong consistency between gamma spectrometry and ICP-MS for the detection of uranium-238, confirming no significant discrepancies between early and late-chain measurements across most samples. Notably, the early-stage uranium-238 isotope concentrations measured via ICP-MS are generally consistent with late-chain uranium-238 isotopes measured via gamma spectrometry. This suggests no significant disequilibrium in most samples.

However, exceptions were found in certain samples, specifically \texttt{PTFE8}, \texttt{PTFE7p}, and \texttt{PTFE9p}, where significant discrepancies between early and late-chain uranium-238 were observed. This indicates a potential break in secular equilibrium, likely due to chemical processes involved in manufacturing these materials.

\subsection{Sample-Specific Findings}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PTFE1}: Showed low contamination levels, with upper limits for both uranium-238 and thorium-232 in gamma spectrometry and ICP-MS measurements, making it a strong candidate for low-background experiments.
    
    \item \textbf{PTFE2}: Similarly exhibited low radiopurity concerns, with low levels of contamination detected via both methods. Thorium-232 remained below detectable levels, further indicating its suitability for sensitive detector applications.
    
    \item \textbf{PTFE8}: Significant uranium-238 contamination was found in the late decay chain, with concentrations of radium-226 exceeding early-chain uranium-238 levels by approximately two orders of magnitude. This sample exhibited a clear break in secular equilibrium, highlighting its unsuitability for applications requiring high radiopurity.
    
    \item \textbf{PTFE10}: Demonstrated very low contamination levels, especially for uranium and thorium, with minimal contributions from radiogenic backgrounds. This sample was the cleanest in the study and is an ideal candidate for ultra-low-background experiments.
    
    \item \textbf{PTFE6p}: While showing higher contamination levels than PTFE1 and PTFE10, this powder sample was found to be relatively pure, with minor deviations between ICP-MS and gamma spectrometry results for thorium-232.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Impact of Manufacturing Processes}
The study reveals that PTFE samples processed under clean conditions (e.g., PTFE10) show much lower levels of radioactive contamination than those produced using standard methods (e.g., PTFE8). This highlights the impact of the PTFE production process on radiopurity and emphasizes the need for careful selection of materials based on their manufacturing history.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Radiopurity and its Implications for DARWIN}
For rare-event searches like the DARWIN experiment, the presence of radioactive contaminants in detector materials can significantly affect sensitivity. In particular, radiogenic neutrons from uranium and thorium decay chains can mimic the nuclear recoil signatures of WIMP interactions, adding an irreducible background. Thus, stringent control over material radiopurity is necessary to reduce these backgrounds below the expected signal from astrophysical neutrinos.

The results from this study provide valuable insights into the radiopurity of commercially available PTFE. Samples such as \texttt{PTFE1} and \texttt{PTFE10} exhibit contamination levels well within the acceptable range for DARWIN, where background from radiogenic neutrons must account for less than 10\% of the total expected background. In contrast, materials like \texttt{PTFE8}, which exhibit significant late-chain uranium-238 contamination, would require substantial purification or alternative material choices to be viable.

\subsection{Secular Equilibrium and Its Influence on Neutron Backgrounds}
One of the key findings of this study is the break in secular equilibrium observed in some samples, particularly \texttt{PTFE8}, \texttt{PTFE7p}, and \texttt{PTFE9p}. These breaks result in disproportionately high levels of radium-226, contributing to a larger radiogenic neutron yield. The presence of disequilibrium can stem from the chemical processing during PTFE manufacture, where certain isotopes may be preferentially removed or concentrated, leading to an uneven distribution of radioisotopes in the material.

For ultra-low-background experiments like DARWIN, where neutron-induced nuclear recoils are a primary source of concern, the selection of materials in secular equilibrium or with minimal disequilibrium is critical. This study suggests that PTFE samples from suppliers that follow stringent processing and handling protocols, such as \texttt{PTFE10}, are better suited for such experiments.

\subsection{Implications for Material Procurement and Future Studies}
This study underscores the necessity for careful material selection and thorough screening for future low-background experiments. The variations in contamination levels observed across different samples, particularly those with a break in secular equilibrium, suggest that a procurement strategy focused on ultrapure PTFE processed in clean environments is essential for minimizing radiogenic backgrounds.

For the DARWIN experiment, the radiopurity requirements are particularly stringent, as discussed in the design sensitivity goals. Future material procurement should prioritize suppliers capable of providing PTFE with contamination levels on par with \texttt{PTFE1} and \texttt{PTFE10}. Furthermore, periodic screening and validation using techniques such as ICP-MS and gamma spectrometry are necessary to ensure that the materials continue to meet the radiopurity standards required for next-generation dark matter and neutrino detectors.
